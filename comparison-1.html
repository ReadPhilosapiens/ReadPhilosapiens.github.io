The Comparison Letters · I

The scoreboard you didn't choose

3 min read · by ParaDox

You can tell someone they're doing well and watch it fail to land. Not because they don't believe you — because you're marking a different exam than the one they think they're sitting.

Watch what happens when you finally get the thing. The salary, the offer, the number, the recognition. For about an afternoon it counts. And then it doesn't. It becomes the floor rather than the ceiling, and the feeling you were actually chasing — the one where you get to stop and be alright — doesn't arrive. You assume it's still ahead somewhere, so you keep going. What you don't notice is that it wasn't ahead the last four times either.

Here's the second scene, and it's stranger. Someone makes you feel behind whose life you would not swap for. Different field, different city, different values entirely. If you laid your priorities side by side, you'd choose yours. And yet something in you registers them as ahead of you — on a track you're not even running.

That's the giveaway. If you're losing a race against someone who isn't in it, the race isn't real. The scoring is.

Here's the tell, and it's clean enough to check right now: you feel behind, but you can't say behind whom, or by when. Try it. Finish the sentence — I am behind ___ by ___. A real deadline has a date. A real race has a finish line and other runners you agreed to run against. If you can't fill in either blank, you're not behind. You're being measured against a rule you never read the terms of.

Because scoreboards get installed early, and almost never by you. A grade that meant approval. A sibling who was the clever one, which quietly made you the other thing. A parent's specific idea of what a serious life looks like. By the time you're old enough to choose your own metrics you already have a full set, and they don't feel like metrics — they feel like reality. Nobody hands you a scoreboard and says this is one option among many. They hand it to you as the way things are.

So here's the one thing to do this week. Write down the metric you're actually using. Not the noble one you'd say out loud if someone asked — the real one, the one running underneath. It usually looks embarrassingly specific when it's written: more money than the people I went to school with. Married before thirty. Respected by a particular kind of person who has never once thought about me.

Then two questions. Who wrote this? And would I have chosen it, if I'd been shown it as one option among several?

Sometimes the answer is yes, and that's genuinely worth knowing — a metric you'd choose again is worth keeping and worth pursuing without guilt. More often the answer is that you inherited it from someone whose life you don't want, at an age when you weren't consulted, and you've been paying it off ever since.

None of this makes wanting to do well a character flaw. Wanting to know how you're doing is reasonable; every functioning person keeps some kind of score. The problem was never that you're scoring. It's that you've been scoring on a card you didn't write, in a game whose rules you couldn't state out loud if someone stopped you and asked.

You're not behind. You're being timed by a clock someone else set, in a race you never agreed to enter.

— ParaDox
