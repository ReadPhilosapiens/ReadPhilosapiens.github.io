The Comparison Letters · III

Enough was never defined

3 min read · by ParaDox

Ask someone what they want and they'll answer immediately. Ask them how much of it would be enough and watch what happens to their face.

Here's the first scene. You hit the number. Whatever yours is — the salary, the follower count, the weight, the client, the flat. You had it in mind for two years. And you get there, and it registers for roughly an afternoon, and then it flattens into the new normal so smoothly that within a fortnight you'd struggle to remember what it felt like to not have it. The relief you were owed doesn't arrive. So you assume you aimed too low, and you set a higher one, and you feel briefly clear-headed about it — because at least now you have a target again.

The second scene is quieter and happens if you sit still. You try to answer the question honestly — what would be enough? — and you find you can't. Not because the answer is complicated. Because there isn't one. What comes back is more, or I'd know it when I got there, which is the same sentence wearing a coat.

Here's the tell: you can state precisely what you want more of, and not at all how much would be enough. Those two things feel like they belong to the same thought, but only one of them has ever been written down. And an undefined quantity cannot be satisfied — not because you're greedy, not because you lack gratitude, but because there is no line to cross. You are trying to finish something that has no finish.

This is what makes it different from ordinary ambition, and worth taking seriously. Ambition with a defined target is a project. Ambition without one is a treadmill with a very good marketing department. From the inside they feel identical. Both involve effort, discipline, and a sense of direction. Only one of them can ever end.

So here's the one thing to do, and do it in writing, because the writing is the whole mechanism: define enough, with a number and a date. Not a feeling. A number. Enough is X by Y. Put it somewhere you'll find it again.

What this buys you is not modesty. It's a checkpoint. When you reach X, you're now forced to make a conscious decision — I am choosing to move this line, for these reasons — instead of the line sliding another ten meters while you weren't looking and you finding yourself behind again without ever having agreed to it. You are allowed to move it. Most people should, sometimes. But moving a line you can see is a decision. Moving a line you can't is just being moved.

Expect one uncomfortable thing when you write the number down: it will often look small. Smaller than you'd admit to wanting out loud, and much smaller than the amount you've been behaving as though you need. That gap — between the number that would actually do it and the amount you're chasing — is the most useful thing you'll find all week. It's the size of the part that was never about the thing.

And none of this is an argument for wanting less. The drive isn't the problem, and people who tell you to simply want less are usually offering you their scoreboard in place of yours. The problem is only ever undefined drive — the kind that can't tell you when it's done, because it was never given the information.

A target you never wrote down can't be reached. It can only be moved — and it will be, quietly, every time you get close.

— ParaDox
