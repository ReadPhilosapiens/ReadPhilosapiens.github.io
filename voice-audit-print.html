A one-week self-assessment

Catching
it.

You can't out-argue the voice, because it isn't reasoning — it's a recording. You don't win a fight with a recording. You identify it. This week you're not trying to silence anything. You're only trying to catch it in the act often enough to hear whose it is.

Notice

Catch the sentence while it's still ringing, not an hour later.

Quote it

Write it word for word. The exact phrasing is the evidence.

Name it

Ask whose voice it is. Say the name if you can find one.

Sort it

At the end of the week, keep the care. Return the cruelty.

—

The three places it shows up

Watch for these
1 The finish line that moves.

You complete something real. There's half a second where you could feel good about it — and then: you should have done it sooner, anyone could have, don't get comfortable. The line slides another ten meters and you're behind again, in a race you're the only one running.
2 The negotiation that opens when you rest.

You lie down and terms appear. You can rest after the message, after the tidying, after you've done enough. Enough never gets defined, because a moving target keeps you moving. So you get up. And you call it discipline.
3 The accent.

Slow down and actually listen — not obey, listen. There's a cadence. A vocabulary. A particular weight in the pause. You are not writing these lines fresh. You are quoting.
The move

Don't argue with what it's saying. Ask one question instead: whose voice is this?

Before you start

Some of these voices were trying to protect you, clumsily, in the only language they had. You're not here to condemn anyone — including yourself. The people who do this hardest are usually the ones who took feedback seriously and wanted to be good. You didn't fail to build a self. You built one faithfully, out of the materials you were handed.

The Log.

One row each time you catch it. Write the sentence exactly as it arrived — not a summary, not a tidier version. The last column is the one that does the work. Most of these lines were built to protect you from something. Naming what usually explains why the line still runs.

When it fired The sentence, word for word Whose voice What it was protecting me from
If you can't find a name

Not every line traces to a person. Some belong to a younger version of you who was frightened and made a rule to stay safe. That counts — write me, at nineteen. A rule you wrote yourself at fifteen is still a rule you can review at thirty.

What you're building

Ten rows is enough to see a pattern. Most people find two or three voices doing all the talking.

The Sort.

Read back through your log. Nearly every one of those sentences is two things fused together — something that was trying to help you, and something that was just cruel. Separate them. You get to keep one and send the other back.

Keep
The care underneath. The thing it was actually right about, said without the knife.
Return to sender
The cruelty. The part that was never about helping you at all.

The rule I'd write for myself instead
Same situation, same standard if the standard was fair — but in your own voice, written by someone who is actually on your side.
The whole point

Named, the sentence stops being the truth and becomes a quote — and a quote can be examined, dated, and set down.

Run it again in ninety days. The voices don't vanish, but they get quieter once they've been identified — and you'll notice faster the second time.
