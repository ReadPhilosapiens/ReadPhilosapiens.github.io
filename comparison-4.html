The Comparison Letters · IV

The two or three people

3 min read · by ParaDox

You think you're competing with the world. You're not. You're competing with about three people, and if you sit still for a minute you can probably name them.

Here's the first scene, and it's the one that proves it. Someone genuinely extraordinary does something extraordinary — a person at the top of your field, or a billionaire, or an athlete. And you feel almost nothing. Mild interest, maybe admiration. No sting. Meanwhile someone from your course, doing roughly what you do, announces something modest, and it lands in your chest like a physical event. Objectively, one of those achievements is enormous and the other isn't. Your reaction is precisely inverted.

The second scene is the one you can test tonight. Try to think of who you feel behind. Not the abstract sense of behind — the specific one. Faces will come. It'll be a very short list, and the same names will be on it as were on it last year.

Here's the tell: envy is specific, not general. It doesn't fire at the best in the world. It fires at people close enough to you that their result reads as a comment on yours. Same age, same start, same field, same room. Proximity, not merit. The closer someone is to being you, the more their success functions as evidence about what you could have done — and the harder it hits.

Which means the thing you've been treating as a vague, oceanic sense of inadequacy is not vague at all. It has a cast list. Two or three people, doing a job that neither of you agreed to.

So here's the one thing to do this week: keep a ledger. Every time it fires — the scroll-stop, the tightening, the fast reflexive assessment — write the name. Just the name and the date. Don't analyse it, don't argue with it, don't feel bad about it. Collect it. Give it seven days.

What comes back is almost always the same two or three names, over and over, and often people you'd describe as friends. That's not a moral emergency and it doesn't make you disloyal. It's a map. It shows you exactly where you believe your identity is at stake — because you only compare where you think the comparison is fair, and you only think it's fair where you believe you were running the same race.

Once you can see the cast, two things become possible that weren't before. You can stop treating a feeling about three specific people as a verdict on your entire life, which is what it's been masquerading as. And you can look at each one and ask the actually interesting question: what do I think their result says about me? The answer is usually a sentence that's easy to argue with once it's out in the open, and impossible to argue with while it's still just a weight in your chest.

None of this happens because you're competitive or bitter. It happens because comparison requires overlap, and your brain is doing something quite sensible — using the people most like you as the closest available measure of what was possible. It's a reasonable instrument pointed at a question it can't actually answer, because those people are not you, didn't start where you started, and aren't running the race you think you're both in.

You're not behind the world. You're behind about three people — and you're the one who put them on the list.

— ParaDox
